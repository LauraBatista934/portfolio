01 / About

Hi, I'm Laura.

I'm a UX/UI designer from Porto Alegre, Brazil. I came to design through words and products, and I still start every screen with the sentence it needs to say.

Before I was drawing interfaces, I was writing for the internet and managing social channels during a very difficult moment for a travel company. That taught me something Figma can't: people don't read screens, they scan for the thing they came for.

So I work from the content out. Hierarchy first, then layout, then the small parts nobody plans for: the empty table, the field that fails, the sentence after the button. I like interfaces that are quiet enough to be understood on the first try.

I'm finishing a degree in Systems Analysis and Development. It means I can read code, talk edge cases with developers, and build a prototype in HTML when Figma isn't the right tool for the question.

How I work four habits, no ceremony
01

Ask first

What is this person actually trying to do? I write it down and re-read the brief. Half of my good decisions came from that second read.

02

Structure

Flows and wireframes until the path is obvious. Wireframes are cheap, and I use them to kill weak ideas before they get beautiful.

03

Craft

Type, spacing, colour, states. I design the empty and the error versions too, because that is where products usually fall apart.

04

Ship

Handoff with real specs, then sit with the developers. Design isn't done when the file is done. It's done when it works.

Instituto Eldorado is a research and development institute in Porto Alegre. Inside, equipment, licences and documents were tracked across spreadsheets, emails and memory. If you needed the projector, you asked around. If you needed to know who had it last, you asked three people.

“Design a clear and intuitive internal platform to manage the company's assets.”
The brief, in the client's words
What I did
  • Persona and benchmark. Studied the people who would use this every day, then compared a few asset management tools to see which patterns they already knew.
  • Flow. Mapped sign in, registration, management and history, including the paths that fail: no results, duplicate asset, wrong permissions.
  • Screens. Designed the three core screens in Figma with one shared component set: inputs, tables, filters, status tags.
  • Validation. Presented to the client, reviewed edge cases with front-end and QA, adjusted before handoff.

02

We met the client in week three. The requirements moved after that meeting and part of the flow had to be rebuilt. Next time I'd push for that conversation before the first wireframe, even when the brief looks complete.

Case 02 / UI design / What the screens had to do

Pretty is the easy part. The states are the work.

Five screens, five jobs
Default feedReading, without a single image to break the rhythm.
WritingThe composer opens over the feed. Writing stays in context.
EditingSame composer, filled. You always know what you are changing.
DeletingOne confirmation, no red flood. Destructive doesn't need to shout.
Empty state“You're all caught up.” A dead end that still says something human.
What I took from it

A feed is a typographic problem before it is a layout problem. Once the hierarchy of title, body and metadata was right, almost every screen solved itself.

Three decisions I can defend
01

Serif for people, sans for the app

What someone wrote is set in a serif, so it reads like writing. Buttons, labels and timestamps stay in sans, so you always know which voice is the app's.

02

One colour, for actions only

The accent blue appears on things you can press and nowhere else. Everything else is a shade of navy. Colour becomes a signpost, not decoration.

03

Dark by default

The product is for reading at night, in bed, with the phone close to your face. Deep navy instead of pure black keeps the contrast soft.

04

Design the aftermath

Delete, empty, edit. Three of the five screens exist for moments the product is missing something or about to lose it. That ratio is on purpose.

Case 03 / Communication / Egali, 2021 to 2022

When the comments turned hostile, someone had to answer them.

Role
Social media and content
Company
Egali Intercâmbio
Context
Post pandemic, delayed programs
Scope
All public channels

Egali sells exchange programs. After the pandemic, a lot of students were still waiting for trips that kept being postponed. The frustration landed on social media, in public, every single day, and it was aimed at whoever answered first.

I was that person. I managed the channels and the tone of the brand during the worst of it.

What the job actually was
  • Answer, publicly. Replies in the comments and in direct messages, in a tone that stayed calm when the comment wasn't.
  • Say only what was true. I checked with operations before promising a date or a refund. Saying “I don't know yet, and here's when you'll hear from us” cost less than a confident lie.
  • Change what we published. The calendar moved from selling trips to explaining delays. Promotion paused until the queue was under control.
  • Protect the people replying. Wrote short guidelines so whoever was on the channel that day answered the same way.

I still write the replies before I write the captions. Tone is a design decision, and most brands only notice it when it breaks.

The situation

Hundreds of public complaints, a queue of students without dates, and a brand that had gone quiet because nobody knew what to say.

What I changed

From silence to named, dated answers. From promotional content to information people could use.

What happened

The threads stopped escalating. People got answers, even when the answer was “not yet”. Leadership recognised the work internally.

“People don't stay angry at a brand that answers them clearly. They stay angry at silence.”
